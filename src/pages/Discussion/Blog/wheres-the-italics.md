<p>Have you ever thought about the <em>italicised</em> words in your Bible? Well, it’s common in any kind of translation to discover that differences in both vocabulary and grammar between the source language and the target language don’t allow each single source word to be translated by a single target word.</p>

<p>Sometimes there’s a Hebrew or Greek word that we can’t properly translate into English, e.g., When Jesus sees Simon and Andrew fishing, Mark 1:17 says: <em>And the Jesus said to them</em>..., but we can’t put ‘the’ before Jesus in that context, so virtually all translations (including those that claim to be literal translations) just quietly drop that word. (The <em>Open English Translation Literal Version</em> is more literal than most other literal versions, and displays <s>the</s> to indicate the dropped Greek word: see <a href="https://Freely-Given.org/OBD/par/MRK/C1V17.htm#Top"><u>https://Freely-Given.org/OBD/par/MRK/C1V17.htm</u></a>.)</p>

<p>But in Mrk 1:19, Jesus sees James (well, Jacob actually but that’s another story), <strong>the of Zebedee</strong> and his brother in a boat preparing their nets. No, that’s not a typo—the word ‘son’ (or ‘child’ or ‘offspring’ or ‘descendant’ or ‘one’) isn’t there in the Greek—it’s implied! So the KJB and most translations add a word to help the English reader. In the 1500’s, italic type was invented to be softer, more like handwriting, and Bible publishers took advantage of italics to print added words in order to be transparent and to inform the readers of their decision to add words not in the original text.</p>

<p>However, over the next hundred years or so (according to the <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italic_type"><u>Wikipedia article</u></a> on <em>Italic type</em>), italics became used for <em>emphasis</em>. So now<strong> the added words </strong>(which should really be <strong>de-emphasised </strong>because they’re <strong>not</strong> in the original text)<strong> appear as <em>emphasised</em> words! </strong>And since then, Bible edition after edition has continued to use <em>italics</em> when the purpose of <em>modern</em> italics is exactly the opposite of what should happen with added words! Oh dear!</p>

<p>But not any more. The new, radical, <em>Open English Translation</em> of the Bible shows added words in <span style="color: #666666">grey</span>, so they’re marked to help the reader know they’re added, yet appropriately <span style="color: #808080">de-emphasised</span> as they should be.</p>
